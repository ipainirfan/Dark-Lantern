On 1 September 1859, astronomer Richard Carrington saw two blinding white flashes on the Sun. Less than a day later, the biggest solar storm ever recorded hit Earth.

What happened:
🌌 Auroras glowed as far south as Cuba and Hawaii.
🍳 Gold miners in the Rocky Mountains woke at 1am and started making breakfast – they thought it was dawn.
⚡ Telegraph wires threw sparks, operators got shocks, paper caught fire.
📡 Between Boston and Portland, operators unplugged their batteries and kept sending messages on storm power alone.
☄️ July 2012 – a blast just as powerful crossed Earth's orbit about 9 days after we had passed.
🌍 A direct hit today could knock out power grids, satellites and GPS for months. Scientists say it will happen again.

SOURCES (fact check)
- R. C. Carrington, "Description of a Singular Appearance seen in the Sun on September 1, 1859", Monthly Notices of the RAS 20, 13 (1859) – two white-light patches, faded within ~5 minutes. Drawing: public domain (Wikimedia Commons, "Carrington Richard drawing of 1859 sunspots").
- Cliver & Svalgaard, Solar Physics 224 (2004) – CME arrival ~17.6 hours after the flare.
- E. Loomis, American Journal of Science (1860–61) – auroras at low latitudes (Cuba, Hawaii); telegraph sparks, shocks, burning paper; Boston–Portland line worked ~2 hours with batteries disconnected.
- Rocky Mountain News, 1859 – miners got up and began breakfast thinking it was morning.
- Baker et al., Space Weather 11 (2013); NASA Science "Near Miss: The Solar Superstorm of July 2012" (2014) – 23 July 2012 CME comparable to Carrington, crossed Earth's orbit ~9 days after Earth passed.
- National Academies, "Severe Space Weather Events" (2008); Lloyd's, "Solar storm risk to the North American electric grid" (2013) – outages lasting months possible.
