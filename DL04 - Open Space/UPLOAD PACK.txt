You'd Have 15 SECONDS in Space Without a Suit 😳 #shorts

Description:
Thrown into space without a suit, you'd have about 15 seconds. Not to live. To stay awake.

What really happens:
💨 You don't explode. Your skin holds you together. But you must breathe out, or the air in your lungs expands and can tear them.
💧 With almost no pressure, the water in your body boils even at 37°C (ebullism), and your skin swells.
🧪 1966 – NASA tester Jim LeBlanc's suit leaked inside a vacuum chamber. He blacked out in about 14 seconds. His last memory: the saliva on his tongue starting to bubble. He was repressurised and woke up.
⏱️ Scientists estimate you could survive up to ~90 seconds, if you're rescued in time.
🚀 1971 – a valve opened on Soyuz 11. Three cosmonauts were lost. They are still the only people to die in space.

SOURCES (fact check)
- NASA (Ask an Astronomer / NASA technical reports): about 10–15 s of useful consciousness in vacuum; body does not explode; holding breath risks lung rupture (pulmonary barotrauma).
- Ebullism: below ~6.3 kPa (Armstrong limit) body fluids boil at 37°C; tissue swells (Billings, "Barometric pressure", Bioastronautics Data Book, NASA SP-3006, 1973).
- Jim LeBlanc, NASA Manned Spacecraft Center, Houston, 14 Dec 1966 – suit depressurised in vacuum chamber; conscious ~14 s; recalled saliva bubbling on his tongue; repressurised and recovered.
- ~90 s survivable with prompt recompression – based on animal studies summarised in NASA SP-3006 (1973).
- Soyuz 11, 30 June 1971 – a pressure-equalisation valve opened at module separation; Dobrovolsky, Volkov and Patsayev died; the only human deaths above the 100 km Kármán line.
